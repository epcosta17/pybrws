Imitating browsers without browsers

An HTTP client in Python where the fingerprint is a parameter rather than a side effect of whichever TLS library you happened to import.

Ernesto Pérez Costa · October 2026

A server decides what you are from bytes you send before any HTTP exists. Not your headers — the shape of your TLS handshake, and then the shape of the HTTP itself. A Python client with perfect browser headers announces itself as a Python client in the first packet it ever sends.

So I wrote the handshake. TLS 1.2 and 1.3 from scratch, then HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 over QUIC, with profiles for Firefox 157 and Chrome 155 built from my own packet captures. These three write-ups are what the measuring turned up.

PART ONE

Cloning a Firefox ClientHello, byte for byte

1,901 bytes, 70% of them a single extension. What varies between captures and what must not. Why a key share cannot be copied. And the afternoon I discovered my own antivirus was replacing the fingerprint of every request I made.

PART TWO

Chrome shuffles, and a question I could not answer

A drawn extension order, GREASE pinned at both ends, and a one-in-sixteen collision that produced an invalid hello. Then six hypotheses eliminated on a target that still refused us — and the answer sitting in a sentence I had already been given.

PART THREE

One browser, two fingerprinting rules

Firefox keeps a fixed extension order over TCP and shuffles it over QUIC, because NSS and neqo are different programs. Read out of Initial packets, which anyone can decrypt. Plus the three conclusions I had to take back.

What the client is

import pybrws
r = pybrws.get("https://example.com", profile="chrome155")
LayerWhat is in it
TLS1.2 and 1.3 — handshake, records, key schedule, resumption, HelloRetryRequest, client certificates
TransportHTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, HTTP/3 over QUIC — each sending its profile's own settings, frame order and header order
Plumbingcookies, sessions, connection pool, proxies, redirects, streaming, content decoding
ProfilesFirefox 157 over TCP, Firefox 157 over QUIC, Chrome 155 — each measured separately

Built on two libraries and nothing else: cryptography for the primitives and aioquic for QUIC's transport. Every byte whose shape is a fingerprint is written here.

It does not run JavaScript, so a "checking your browser" page is out of reach by design, not by accident. That boundary matters and it comes up in part two: a JavaScript challenge challenges a real browser too, and the browser passes it by executing the script. What a fingerprint decides is one layer earlier — whether you are sent there at all.

On method

Everything here was measured rather than recalled, and the uncomfortable half of that is the retractions. Several findings in these posts are things I published to my own notebook and then had to take back when more rounds dissolved them, or when a column I had not thought to print turned out to contain the answer.

They are included on purpose. A client that works is worth less than a client whose author can tell you why it works and where the evidence stops.
